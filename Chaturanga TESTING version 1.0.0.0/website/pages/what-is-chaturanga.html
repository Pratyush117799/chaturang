What is Chaturanga?

The ancient Indian war game that gave birth to Chess, Xiangqi, and Shogi — and shaped 1,500 years of strategic thinking.

Chaturanga is a 6th-century CE Indian board game and the oldest known ancestor of the chess family of games. Every version of chess played today — from Western chess to Chinese Xiangqi to Japanese Shogi — descends, directly or indirectly, from Chaturanga.

The Meaning of the Word

Chaturanga (चतुरंग) is a compound Sanskrit word:

  • Chatur (चतुर) — four
  • Anga (अंग) — division, limb, or part

Together: "four divisions." This refers specifically to the four arms (angas) of the ancient Indian military described in texts like the Arthashastra of Chanakya: the war elephant (Gaja), the cavalry (Ashva), the war chariot (Ratha), and the infantry (Pada). These four military branches became the four piece types of the game.

The word Chaturanga also appears in the Mahabharata to describe the fully-arrayed formation of an army, reinforcing its deep connection to Indian military culture.

Historical Origins

The precise origin of Chaturanga is a subject of ongoing scholarly debate. The most widely accepted dating places it in 6th-century CE India during the Gupta Empire — one of the golden ages of Indian science, literature, and culture.

The earliest surviving written mention of a game called Chaturanga appears in the Harshacharita (~625 CE), a biography of the Emperor Harsha written by the Sanskrit poet Bāṇabhaṭṭa. Later references appear in the Vishnu Purana and various Sanskrit texts describing court games.

Some historians have argued for an earlier origin — as far back as 2nd–4th century CE — based on descriptions of dice and piece-based war games in earlier Sanskrit literature, though these attributions are contested.

"The game of Chaturanga was played with dice and four types of pieces — representing the four divisions of the ancient Indian army — on a board of 64 squares." — Based on reconstructed historical rules from Sanskrit texts

Gaja · Elephant

गज

The war elephant — the mightiest of the four. Leaps exactly two squares diagonally. In modern chess, this became the Bishop (which slides diagonally instead of leaping).

Ashva · Horse

अश्व

The cavalry horse. Moves in an L-shape — identical to the Knight in modern chess, which it directly became. One of the most continuous piece movements across all chess variants.

⚔️

Ratha · Chariot

रथ

The war chariot — the fastest piece. Moves any number of squares horizontally or vertically, exactly like the Rook in modern chess. The Rook's shape itself may derive from the chariot wheel.

🪖

Pada · Foot Soldier

पद

The infantry. Moves one square forward and captures diagonally — identical to the Pawn in modern chess. On promotion (reaching the far end), it typically becomes a Ratha.

👑

Raja · King

राज

The ruler. Moves one square in any direction — identical to the King in all modern chess variants. The Raja's capture (not checkmate) ends the game in many Chaturanga variants.

How It Was Played

Chaturanga was played on an 8×8 Ashtāpada board — the same grid used in modern chess. The game's defining mechanic was the Pasha (dice): at the start of each turn, a player rolled a four-sided or six-sided die. The result determined which piece type was permitted to move that turn.

  • Roll of 1: Must move a Pada (foot soldier)
  • Roll of 2: Must move an Ashva (horse)
  • Roll of 3: Must move a Gaja (elephant)
  • Roll of 4: Must move the Raja (king) or Ratha (chariot)

This dice mechanic transformed Chaturanga from a purely deterministic game of perfect information into a probabilistic strategy game — the challenge lay not just in planning moves, but in formulating flexible strategies that could adapt to any dice result. If a player had no legal move for the piece type indicated by the dice, the turn was typically forfeited.

Victory was achieved by capturing the opponent's Raja. In some recorded variants, a player could also win by eliminating all of the opponent's pieces (excluding the Raja) — a condition called bare king. Stalemate, in many versions, was a win rather than a draw, inverting the rule of modern chess.

How Chaturanga Spread Across the World

~6th Century CE · India

Chaturanga

The original game emerges in Gupta-era India. Four piece types, 8×8 board, dice-based movement. Played across the Indian subcontinent and documented in Sanskrit literature.

~6th–7th Century CE · Persia

Chatrang / Shatranj

Chaturanga reaches the Sasanian Persian Empire, where it is called Chatrang and later Shatranj. The dice are dropped; the game becomes fully deterministic. The Karnamak-i Ardeshir and Wizishn i chatrang texts describe this transition.

~9th–10th Century CE · Arab World

Shatranj in the Arab World

Arab scholars and traders adopt Shatranj enthusiastically. The first chess treatises — by Al-Adli, As-Suli, and Al-Lajlaj — are written in Arabic. The game spreads throughout the Islamic world and into North Africa and Spain.

~10th–15th Century CE · Europe

Medieval Chess → Modern Chess

Chess enters Europe via Spain and Sicily. By the 15th century, European players dramatically modify piece movements: the Queen gains sweeping diagonal and orthogonal movement, the Bishop gains its sliding diagonal move, and the game reaches its modern form.

~10th–12th Century CE · China

Xiangqi (Chinese Chess)

A related chess variant develops in China, incorporating distinctly Chinese elements: the River, the Palace, the Cannon piece. Whether from direct Indian influence or parallel development via Central Asian intermediaries remains debated.

~12th Century CE · Japan

Shogi (Japanese Chess)

Shogi develops in Japan, likely from Chinese and Korean intermediaries. Its most distinctive feature — the ability to re-deploy captured pieces — makes it unique among the chess family.

Chaturanga vs. Modern Chess

While Chaturanga and modern chess share the same 8×8 board and the same basic piece types, they are strategically very different games:

  • Dice vs. determinism: Chaturanga uses Pasha (dice); chess does not. This is the single largest strategic difference — Chaturanga rewards adaptive thinking under uncertainty.
  • Elephant movement: The Gaja in Chaturanga leaps exactly two squares diagonally (a fixed leap, not a slide). The modern Bishop slides any number of squares diagonally, making it far more powerful.
  • Win condition: Chaturanga ends on king capture; modern chess ends on checkmate (threatening the king, not capturing it).
  • Players: Chaturanga supports 2 or 4 players; chess is always 2.
  • Stalemate: In many Chaturanga variants, stalemating the opponent is a win — the opposite of modern chess.
