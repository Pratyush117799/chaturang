# Chaturanga — When Pawns Become Kings (And You Choose Where)

*How the world's oldest strategy game plays by rules modern chess abandoned*

---

Modern chess is 1,500 years younger than Chaturanga. And somewhere in those centuries of evolution, the game lost something remarkable: a rule where promoting a pawn doesn't just transform the piece — it grants you the **strategic freedom to place your new King anywhere you choose** on the board.

Chaturanga, the ancient Indian four-player predecessor to chess, carries rules that seem alien to modern players but reveal a deeper, more flexible strategic philosophy. Here are the most distinctive rules that set it apart.

---

## 1. The Pāśaka — Dice That Command Your Army

The very first difference: **you don't choose which piece to move**. Before every turn, a player rolls the Pāśaka (ancient Indian dice). The number rolled determines *which category of piece* you are obligated to move:

| Roll | Piece |
|---|---|
| 1 | Ratha (Chariot) |
| 2 or 5 | Any piece (Free Choice) |
| 3 | Ashwa (Knight/Horse) |
| 4 | Danti (Elephant) |
| 6 | Nara (Pawn) or Raja (King) |

If you roll a number but have **no legal moves** for that piece type, your turn is automatically forfeited. This probabilistic element transforms Chaturanga into a game of **adapting to uncertainty** — your plan must account for the possibility that next turn you may be forced to move a piece you didn't intend to.

This is fundamentally different from modern chess, where every turn you have full freedom to move any piece. In Chaturanga, the dice is as much a player as you are.

---

## 2. Nara Promotion — Column Determines Destiny

In modern chess, any pawn that reaches the 8th rank can become a Queen (or any other piece). The promotion piece and destination are entirely in the player's hands.

In Chaturanga, the Nara's (pawn's) promotion is **fixed by the column it reaches**. Each column corresponds to a specific piece based on what piece originally occupied that column's back rank:

| Column | Piece it Promotes To |
|---|---|
| **a** or **h** (Ratha's column) | **Ratha** (Chariot) |
| **b** or **g** (Ashwa's column) | **Ashwa** (Knight) |
| **c** or **f** (Danti's column) | **Danti** (Elephant) |
| **d** or **e** (Raja's column) | **Raja** (King) |

The column is the Nara's destiny. A Nara marching up the **a-file** becomes a Ratha. A Nara threading its way to the **d-file** back rank becomes a **Raja** — and this is where Chaturanga's most remarkable rule emerges.

---

## 3. The Raja Promotion Exception — A King Placed Anywhere

When a Nara reaches the Ratha, Ashwa, or Danti column's back rank, it **immediately becomes that piece on the same square**. The transformation is automatic and in place.

But when a Nara reaches the **Raja column**, the rules change completely:

> **The end square is cleared. The new Raja is NOT placed on the promotion square. Instead, the player may place their new Raja on ANY vacant square on the entire board.**

This single rule difference between Raja promotion and all other promotions has profound strategic implications:

- A player can march a Nara to the Raja column as a **long-range plan** to place a King anywhere they choose.
- The new Raja can be placed adjacent to your own pieces for immediate safety, or deep in enemy territory for a surprising counter-attack.
- Unlike Ratha, Ashwa and Danti which are confined to the promotion square, a promoted Raja appears wherever you strategically need a King.

This is not a minor rule variation — it reflects a **fundamentally different philosophy** about what a King represents in the game. A Raja is not just another piece; its placement is a statement of strategic intent.

---

## 4. The Raja Can Return — Ally Capture Revival

In modern chess, once a King is captured the game is over. In Chaturanga, which is played in teams of two (2v2), the game continues even after a King is taken — because the Raja can **come back**.

There are two paths to revival:

1. **Nara Promotion Revival:** If a Nara reaches the back rank in the Raja column, the new Raja can be placed on any vacant square.
2. **Teammate Capture Revival:** If your **teammate** captures one of the **opponent's Kings**, your previously captured Raja is revived and you may place it on any vacant square.

In both cases, placement is free — **any vacant square on the board**.

---

## 5. The Guarded Rule — Minor Pieces Cannot Capture Royalty

Another striking departure from modern chess: in Chaturanga, **minor pieces (Ashwa and Danti) cannot capture a Ratha or Raja**. These pieces are "guarded" — visible, but legally untouchable by minor pieces.

This gives the Ratha (Chariot) and Raja (King) a powerful immunity. Only another Ratha, Raja, or Nara can threaten them.

---

## 6. No Queen, No Castling, No En Passant

Three of modern chess's most powerful concepts are simply **absent** in Chaturanga:

- **No Queen** — The Ratha is the most powerful mobile piece but can only move orthogonally.
- **No Castling** — The Raja must fight its way to safety.
- **No En Passant** — Naras capture only by standard diagonal captures.

These absences reveal how modern chess evolved by *adding* power over the centuries. Chaturanga's measured pace reflects a different kind of strategic depth.

---

## Why These Rules Matter

Chaturanga's rules are not arbitrary quirks of an ancient game. They reflect a sophisticated design philosophy:

- **Dice mechanics** introduce controlled uncertainty — both players see the roll and must adapt.
- **Column-based promotion** means a Nara's strategic value depends on *where* it travels, not just how far.
- **Raja placement on promotion** rewards long-term planning with a high-value tactical reward.
- **Ally revival** rewards coordination and team play.

Together, these rules create a game that is simultaneously simpler in some ways and deeper in others — a living connection to how ancient strategists thought about war, probability, and the nature of a King.
