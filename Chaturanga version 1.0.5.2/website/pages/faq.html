About Chaturanga

Chaturanga is an ancient Indian board game originating around the 6th century CE. It is widely regarded as the ancestor of all modern chess variants — including Chess, Xiangqi (Chinese Chess), and Shogi (Japanese Chess). Played on an 8×8 grid called the Ashtāpada, it featured four types of pieces representing the four arms of the ancient Indian military: the elephant, horse, chariot, and foot soldier. A defining feature of Chaturanga is the use of dice (Pasha) to determine which piece may move each turn — introducing probability as a strategic element absent from modern chess.

Chaturanga is a Sanskrit compound word: Chatur (four) + Anga (division/limb). Together it means "four divisions" — specifically the four arms of the ancient Indian military: the Gaja (war elephant), the Ashva (horse/cavalry), the Ratha (war chariot), and the Pada (foot soldier). These four units became the basis for the game's four piece types.

The exact origin is debated among historians, but most scholarly consensus places Chaturanga in 6th century CE India (Gupta period). The earliest written reference appears in the Harshacharita (~625 CE) by the poet Bāṇabhaṭṭa. Some historians argue for an even earlier origin, possibly 2nd–4th century CE, based on other textual and archaeological evidence.

Yes — Chaturanga is the most widely accepted historical ancestor of modern chess. It spread westward from India to Persia, where it became Chatrang and then Shatranj. Arab traders and scholars brought Shatranj to medieval Europe, where it gradually evolved into the game of chess we know today. Simultaneously, Chaturanga spread eastward through Central Asia into China, giving rise to Xiangqi, and further east into Japan as Shogi. See our full history article for more.

Chaturanga is played on an 8×8 board. At the start of each turn, a player rolls the Pasha (dice) — the result determines which piece type may be moved that turn. Each piece moves in a specific way: the Ratha (chariot) moves like a rook in chess; the Ashva (horse) moves like a knight; the Gaja (elephant) leaps two squares diagonally; the Pada (foot soldier) moves forward one square and captures diagonally; and the Raja (king) moves one square in any direction. The goal is to capture the opponent's Raja. Visit our How to Play page for a full interactive guide.

Historically, Chaturanga was played by four players, each commanding one side of the board — representing the four divisions of an army. However, a 2-player variant also exists and is historically documented. Our online implementation supports both 2-player and 4-player modes.

Chaturanga vs. Chess vs. Xiangqi

The key differences: (1) Dice: Chaturanga uses Pasha (dice) to determine which piece type can move each turn; chess has no dice. (2) Piece movement: The elephant in Chaturanga leaps exactly two squares diagonally (unlike the bishop, which slides). (3) Players: Chaturanga supports up to 4 players; chess is always 2. (4) Win condition: In some Chaturanga variants, the goal is to capture — not merely threaten — the king. See our full comparison article.

Xiangqi evolved from games in the same Chaturanga lineage but developed its own unique features over centuries: a 9×10 board vs Chaturanga's 8×8; a River dividing the board that restricts certain pieces; a Palace (3×3 zone) restricting the General/King; unique pieces like the Cannon (must jump to capture); and pieces placed on intersections rather than squares. Both games use a king and four piece types, but they play very differently. Read more in our comparison guide.

Chaturanga came first, originating in India around the 6th century CE. Modern chess developed from it over several centuries, reaching Europe by around the 9th–10th century CE and taking its current form by the 15th century. Xiangqi's exact origins are debated — some Chinese historians argue for an independent origin, but most comparative game historians trace it to the same Indian/Central Asian lineage, with its distinctive features emerging by the 10th–12th century CE.

For Western chess players, Xiangqi has a moderate learning curve. The board and piece count are similar to chess, but the unique pieces (Cannon, Advisor, Elephant restricted to home half) and the Palace/River mechanics require adjustment. Many chess players report being able to play basic Xiangqi within an hour but finding it takes weeks to develop sound strategy. Chaturanga, by contrast, adds the complexity of dice-based movement — making strategic planning probabilistic rather than deterministic.

About Xiangqi (Chinese Chess)

Xiangqi (象棋), commonly called Chinese Chess, is one of the world's most widely played board games with an estimated 200–500 million players, predominantly in China and across East and Southeast Asia. It is played on a 9×10 grid, with each side controlling 16 pieces across 7 types: General, Advisor, Elephant, Horse, Chariot, Cannon, and Soldier. The Cannon — which must jump over exactly one piece to capture — is Xiangqi's most distinctive piece.

The River is a boundary running across the middle of the Xiangqi board, dividing it into two sides. Some pieces — notably the Elephant and Advisor — cannot cross the River and are confined to their own side. The Palace (also called the Fortress) is a marked 3×3 section on each side of the board. The General (king equivalent) and Advisors must stay within the Palace at all times. Additionally, the two opposing Generals can never face each other directly on the same open file — a rule called the "Flying General."

By raw player count, Xiangqi may actually rival or exceed chess. Estimates suggest 200–500 million Xiangqi players, mostly in China, Vietnam, and the Chinese diaspora worldwide, compared to approximately 600–700 million chess players globally. However, in terms of international competitive infrastructure, global media presence, and online platform development, chess currently has the broader reach. Both games have thriving competitive scenes.
